Abstract 
This document presents numerical simulation results of a local hidden-variable model inspired by 
hydrodynamic quantum analogs of the “walking droplet” type. Unlike standard pilot-wave approaches in 
which the guiding field influences the particle without being affected in return, we implement here a 
continuous bidirectional coupling: the particle acts as a moving source that generates its own pilot field. We 
show that this local deterministic dynamics with stochastic noise is sufficient to produce, without additional 
axioms, the emergence of at least two key statistical features of quantum mechanics: relaxation toward the 
Born distribution (𝜌(𝑥) ≈  |𝜓|²), and a Pauli-type exclusion for two particles interacting through the medium.

2. Results: Emergence of Born’s Rule (1D) 
A single particle is simulated many times over 40,000 time steps. The empirical probability density 𝜌(𝑥), 
obtained from the histogram of particle positions, is compared to the time-averaged field intensity |𝜓𝑒𝑓𝑓|² 
 
Observations: 
• The particle, initially launched at random, relaxes toward an ergodic trajectory that samples the 
eigenmodes of the dynamical cavity it generates. 
• The measured correlation between 𝜌(𝑥),  and |𝜓|² is excellent, with a very small relative L¹ error. 
• These results support the “quantum relaxation” hypothesis: |𝜓|² acts as a natural statistical attractor 
of the chaotic dynamics. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
3. Results: Pauli-Type Exclusion (Two-Body Interaction) 
The model is extended to two identical particles. In each run, two particle pairs are generated: 
• One real pair, capable of interacting indirectly via the deformation of a shared field, 
coupling: 𝜓 =  𝜓1  +  𝜓2 
 
• One “ghost” pair evolving in separate fields and therefore unable to interact. 
Spatial correlations are then analyzed to detect fermion-like signatures. 
No antisymmetrization of the wave function is imposed. The observed exclusion arises solely from 
dynamically induced field-mediated interactions.

Interpretation of Quantitative Differences:  
 The asymmetry observed in the joint position heatmap is not a failure of prediction or a failure of 
exclusion, but a direct consequence of using distinguishable particles with fixed initial labels.  
 
The discrepancy between the measured heatmap (bottom-left) and the theoretical fermionic 
heatmap (bottom-right) arises from particle distinguishability in the simulation. Particles are initialized with 
one on the left (x₁ ≈ −10) and one on the right (x₂ ≈ 10), producing a concentration in that region. 
For joint positions to cross the diagonal, particles must pass through each other to exchange sides. While 
possible, such crossings are suppressed by the exclusion dynamics. 
In contrast, theoretical fermionic heatmaps assume particle indistinguishability: it is fundamentally 
impossible to identify which particle occupies which side. Impossible to know whether the particle located at 
𝑥 ≈ −10  is indeed particle 1, launched to the left, or the other particle that has switched sides. Since the 
particles are indistinguishable, we must assume that they may have swapped places, leading to the 
symmetric distribution with two lobes on both side of the diagonal.

The finite value 𝑔(2)(0)  ≈  0.35 , instead of the ideal 𝑔(2)(0) =  0 , is attributed to classical tunneling 
effects (particles with sufficient kinetic energy to overcome the repulsive barrier induced by the field), and to 
the absence of explicit antisymmetrization. Each crossing (red points on the other side of the heatmap 
diagonal), contributes directly to 𝑔(2)(𝑟).  
 
Coupling mode: 
The coupling mode of the two particles interacting through the shared field can be changed in the 
simulation, switching between two different modes: “sum” or “diff”. 
sum:  𝜓 =  𝜓1  +  𝜓2 
diff:    𝜓 =  𝜓1  −  𝜓2 
The “sum” mode induces the fermionic behavior of exclusion described above, whereas the “diff” mode 
induces a bosonic behavior of bunching. 
Furthermore, mathematically, subtracting is equivalent to adding a wave phase shifted by π 
𝜓 =  𝜓1(𝜑) − 𝜓2(𝜑) =  𝜓1(𝜑) + 𝜓2(𝜑 + 𝜋) 
This transforms the interference pattern from one creating an effective repulsive barrier, to one creating an 
effective attractive well.  
This modeling could provide insights into the mechanisms underlying this quantum behavior and statistics 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
4. Perspectives: Emergent Polarization in 2D 
Preliminary two-dimensional simulations were performed to test the robustness of the model and visualize 
the field structure.  
• Born rule in 2D: The particle correctly samples the 2D field, with 𝜌(𝑥, 𝑦) ≈  |𝜓|² 
• 2D structures: Although 𝜓 is a scalar field, analysis of the probability current 
J = Im(𝜓 ∗ ∇𝜓) reveals local vectorial structures (vortices), suggesting a possible connection with

Conclusion 
These results suggest that Born and Pauli statistics don't necessarily have to be taken as 
fundamental postulates, but may instead emerge from a local and stochastic pilot-wave dynamics with 
feedback. The source code (Python/Numba) is publicly available for replication. 
 
 
 
 
Speculative Interpretation of the Model:  
Importantly, one possible (though not mandatory) interpretation of this model is that it naturally 
allows for a fundamental distinction between fermions and bosons. 
Fermions may be viewed as coupled particle-wave entities that interact together, whereas bosons 
correspond to pure waves that propagate and superpose classically. Fermions emit a bosonic excitation 
during each interaction with their wave field, or even continuously. 
In this model, the polarization of emitted bosons (e.g., photons) can be considered as an emergent property 
of the propagation modes of the field emitted by the source particle. Rather than as a fundamental degree 
of freedom. By analogy, these modes can be likened to transverse or rotational propagation structures, 
evidently dependent on the orientation relative to the detector (somehow also analogous to classical wave 
propagation: P-waves, S-waves, etc.)
